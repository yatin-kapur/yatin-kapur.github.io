Collaboration, Conflict and Negotiation

Prof. Zur Shapira · NYU Stern · MGMT-GB.2159.T1 · August intensive · Aug 18 – 27, 2026 (6 sessions)

Negotiation as "the art and science of securing agreements between two or more parties who are interdependent and who seek to maximize their outcomes." A simulation-driven course: every session pairs a framework lecture with a live negotiation exercise against classmates, most with asymmetric information. The arc runs from claiming value (distributive bargaining) to creating value (integrative bargaining) to multiparty coalition and inter-organizational negotiation.

Course structure: attendance & participation 20%, two post-negotiation reflection reports 40%, take-home final 40% (~8 short essay questions in a single 2.5-hour sitting, focused on applying the frameworks). Text: Bargaining for Advantage, G. Richard Shell (3rd ed., 2018) — "BFA" below.

Key takeaways

Course in progress (Aug 18–27, 2026) — growing as sessions happen.

  1. BATNA is your power source — your Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement determines when you can afford to walk. Improve it before the negotiation; a strong BATNA changes everything at the table.
  2. Know your reservation price, and estimate theirs — your true walk-away point derives from your BATNA. Most preparation is figuring out the other side's.
  3. Deals happen in the ZOPA — the Zone Of Possible Agreement is the overlap between the two reservation prices. Everything inside it is a possible deal; where you land inside it is what distributive bargaining decides.
  4. Anchors work — use them deliberately — first offers pull final outcomes toward them (anchoring & adjustment). Anchor first when you're well informed; when anchored, re-anchor rather than counter incrementally.
  5. Claiming vs. creating value — distributive bargaining divides a fixed pie; integrative bargaining grows it before dividing it.
  6. A "no deal" on price may still be a deal — an apparently empty bargaining zone on a single issue can open up when you add issues beyond price (Texoil's core lesson).
  7. Asymmetric information is the norm — you rarely know the other side's true preferences; strategy is choosing what to reveal and when.
  8. Coalitions change the game — multiparty negotiation adds alignment, sequencing, and fairness dynamics that two-party frameworks don't capture.

Session 1 · Tue Aug 18

Fundamentals: conflict, trust, bargaining zone, BATNA

Simulation: Price negotiation I (D-S, Knight-Excalibur) · Reading: BFA ch. 1–2

The core vocabulary

  • BATNA — Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement: what you actually do if this negotiation fails. It's the source of your leverage, and the thing to invest in before you sit down. Never accept a deal worse than your BATNA.
  • Reservation price — your true walk-away point, derived from your BATNA. Buyer's reservation = the most they'll pay; seller's = the least they'll accept. Keep it private; deciding it in advance protects you from in-the-moment pressure.
  • ZOPA (bargaining zone) — the Zone Of Possible Agreement: the overlap between the buyer's and seller's reservation prices. Positive zone → any point inside is a possible deal, and bargaining decides who captures more of the surplus. Negative zone → no deal exists on this issue alone.
  • Aspiration (target) — the ambitious-but-defensible outcome you aim for. Negotiators with specific, optimistic targets outperform those who only know their walk-away; anchoring on your reservation price makes mediocre deals feel like wins.

Conflict and trust

Negotiation is interdependence: you need the other side to get what you want. Some tension between claiming for yourself and preserving the relationship is inherent — the fundamentals session frames conflict as something to be managed, not avoided, and trust as an asset that changes what information you can safely exchange.

Preparation is the game: most of a negotiation is decided before it starts — know your BATNA, set your reservation price, pick an aspiration, and form a hypothesis about their side of each.
Session 2 · Thu Aug 20

Distributive bargaining, price negotiation II

Simulation: Texoil · Reading: BFA ch. 3–4; Cialdini, "The Science of Persuasion"

Anchoring

  • Anchoring & adjustment — the first number on the table exerts a gravitational pull on the final outcome. People adjust away from an anchor insufficiently, even when they know it's arbitrary.
  • When to make the first offer — anchor first when you're well informed about the ZOPA; let them go first when you're uncertain, since their offer is information (and you avoid anchoring against yourself).
  • Defusing an anchor — don't haggle from their number; re-anchor with your own figure and rationale, or explicitly set the anchor aside before negotiating.
  • Concession patterns — in distributive bargaining, offers are signals: the size and pace of your concessions telegraphs how close you are to your reservation price. Aim high (defensibly), concede slowly and decreasingly.

Texoil: when the bargaining zone looks empty

A gas-station sale where, on price alone, the two sides appear to have no overlap — the seller needs more than the buyer can pay. The lesson: an apparently negative ZOPA on a single issue is an invitation to expand the issue set. Asking about underlying interests (why do they need that number? what do they need it for?) can reveal non-price terms that bridge the gap. Pairs who treat it as a pure price haggle deadlock; pairs who explore interests find a deal.

Positions vs. interests: a position is the number someone states; an interest is the need behind it. Price deadlocks often dissolve when you negotiate over interests.

Cialdini's six principles of persuasion (assigned reading)

  • Reciprocity — people repay what they receive; concessions invite concessions.
  • Commitment & consistency — small commitments create pressure to stay consistent with them.
  • Social proof — people follow what similar others do.
  • Liking — we say yes to people we like; similarity and genuine praise build it.
  • Authority — credentials and expertise carry persuasive weight.
  • Scarcity — opportunities seem more valuable when limited or closing.
