Collaboration, Conflict and Negotiation

Prof. Zur Shapira · NYU Stern · MGMT-GB.2159.T1 · August intensive · Aug 18 – 27, 2026 (6 sessions)

Negotiation as "the art and science of securing agreements between two or more parties who are interdependent and who seek to maximize their outcomes." A simulation-driven course: every session pairs a framework lecture with a live negotiation exercise against classmates, most with asymmetric information. The arc runs from claiming value (distributive bargaining) to creating value (integrative bargaining) to multiparty coalition and inter-organizational negotiation.

Course structure: attendance & participation 20%, two post-negotiation reflection reports 40%, take-home final 40% (~8 short essay questions in a single 2.5-hour sitting, focused on applying the frameworks). Text: Bargaining for Advantage, G. Richard Shell (3rd ed., 2018) — "BFA" below.

Key takeaways

Course in progress (Aug 18–27, 2026) — growing as sessions happen.

  1. BATNA is your power source — your Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement determines when you can afford to walk. Improve it before the negotiation; a strong BATNA changes everything at the table.
  2. Know your reservation price, and estimate theirs — your true walk-away point derives from your BATNA. Most preparation is figuring out the other side's.
  3. Deals happen in the ZOPA — the Zone Of Possible Agreement is the overlap between the two reservation prices. Everything inside it is a possible deal; where you land inside it is what distributive bargaining decides.
  4. Never reveal your reservation price — once known it stops being a bottom line and becomes an anchor the other side expects concessions from.
  5. Anchors work — use them deliberately — first offers pull final outcomes toward them, even experts adjust insufficiently, and even a decoy option nobody picks reshapes choices. Anchor first when you're well informed; when anchored, re-anchor immediately rather than counter incrementally.
  6. Preparation is 70% of the game — success is ~70% away-from-the-table preparation, ~20% information exchange, ~10% bargaining skill. Bring a reference-point arsenal with a story for each.
  7. Frames change choices — people are risk-averse in gains and risk-seeking in losses, so the same offer lands differently depending on the reference point you set. Frame proposals as gains relative to where the other side stands.
  8. Your message is not what you sent — every move gets received, perceived, and interpreted through the other side's lens. Plan what your concession pattern says, not just what it costs.
  9. Claiming vs. creating value — distributive bargaining divides a fixed pie; integrative bargaining grows it before dividing it.
  10. A "no deal" on price may still be a deal — an apparently empty bargaining zone on a single issue can open up when you add issues beyond price (Texoil's core lesson).
  11. Trade on differences — and bet on disagreements — joint gains hide wherever the two sides differ: marginal values, time preferences, risk appetite, forecasts. When you disagree about the future, don't argue over it; structure a contingent bet that both sides value positively. And package issues together — one-at-a-time bargaining forecloses every trade.
  12. Asymmetric information is the norm — you rarely know the other side's true preferences; strategy is choosing what to reveal and when.
  13. Coalitions change the game — multiparty negotiation adds alignment, sequencing, and fairness dynamics that two-party frameworks don't capture.

Session 1 · Tue Aug 18

Fundamentals: conflict, trust, bargaining zone, BATNA

Simulations: Dosagen-Synertech, Knight-Excalibur · Reading: BFA ch. 1–2

Distributive negotiation

The starting model: a single-issue, fixed-sum negotiation where one party's gain is the other's loss — dividing a pie of fixed size. Interests conflict almost directly, and each side tries to maximize its share. (The back half of the course relaxes every one of these assumptions.)

The four key principles

  • BATNA — Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement: what you actually do if this negotiation fails. Your greatest source of power. Evidence from job-offer negotiations: ~50% of candidates with a BATNA got the start date they wanted, vs. ~10% of those without one.
  • Reservation price — your bottom line: the point below which you prefer no agreement. Formally, RP = BATNA ± adjustments — opportunity costs, switching costs, ego, idiosyncratic preferences. Define it before negotiating, and try to learn theirs.
  • Bargaining zone (ZOPA) — the space between the buyer's reservation price (BR) and the seller's (SR). If BR > SR, a positive zone exists and any point in it beats both parties' alternatives; if BR < SR, the zone is negative and no single-issue deal exists.
  • Aspiration level — the optimistic-but-defensible target you actually aim for. Focus on your aspiration, not your reservation price — anchoring on your own bottom line makes mediocre deals feel like wins.

BATNA tips

  • Know it, and think of it in specific terms — a vague "I'll find something else" is not a BATNA.
  • Improve it before you negotiate, and keep options open during.
  • You want the counterpart to believe you have a strong BATNA.
  • Only reveal your BATNA when all three hold: you're nearing impasse, your BATNA is strong, and you still want this deal.

Reservation price discipline

  • Never reveal your reservation price. Once known, it stops being your bottom line and becomes an anchor — the other side expects concessions from it.
  • Don't change it arbitrarily mid-negotiation — that's in-the-moment pressure talking, not analysis.

First offers

  • How high? Fisher & Ury's test: "as high as you can go without embarrassing yourself in front of a respected third party."
  • Learn the market first; only let the other party open when you have no information (their offer is data).
  • If they open first, immediately re-anchor with your own number — don't negotiate from theirs.

Dosagen-Synertech: ZOPA in action

A plant sale between two pharma CFOs. The seller's alternative — strip the plant and sell it piecemeal for ~$17M — sets its reservation price; the buyer's alternative — build new for $25M (and wait a year for FDA approval) — sets the other end. That's a positive $8M bargaining zone, and where a pair lands inside it is pure distributive bargaining. The case is also a lesson in reference points: the same plant supports a $15M story (bought from a bankrupt seller three years ago), a $19M story (appraisal, minus a declining market), and a $26M story (a newer comparable's sale) — each side selectively deploys the reference points that favor it. Knight-Excalibur ran the same distributive logic on a supply-contract negotiation with mark-ups, defect rates, and delivery terms in play.

The 70/20/10 rule: negotiation success is ~70% away-from-the-table preparation, ~20% at-the-table information exchange, and only ~10% bargaining skill. Preparation is the game.

Simulation ground rules for the course: never show your confidential sheet or point schedule, don't invent facts not in the case, and remember the goal is maximizing your own points — not beating your opponent.

Session 2 · Thu Aug 20

Distributive bargaining, price negotiation II

Simulation: Texoil · Reading: BFA ch. 3–4; Cialdini, "The Science of Persuasion"

Setting your reservation price is modeling, not feeling

A reservation price is the minimum-value outcome you'll accept before impasse — and it should come from a model, not a gut number. Identify your alternatives (buyer: alternative investments; seller: alternative sources of cash), run the numbers on what they're worth, and set your bottom line where the current deal equals your best alternative.

  • Be explicit with yourself about the assumptions and trade-offs behind your RP.
  • Don't violate it arbitrarily mid-bargain — take a time-out before agreeing to any outcome that breaches it.
  • In prolonged negotiations, consciously (and explicitly) update it as facts change.
  • Anchor yourself upward too: set a reasonable aspiration, and keep a mental picture of the whole bargaining zone during the negotiation.

Opening offers and reference points

  • Open outside the zone in your favor, but within reason — not too wide, and never inside the ZOPA (that gives away surplus for free).
  • Open aggressively: high if selling, low if buying; make concessions reciprocal, never unilateral.
  • Build a reference-point arsenal before you sit down: market price, list price, what they/you paid, your budget, another bid, "fair" price, reasonable ROI — with a prepared story for each. Deploy the favorable ones; be ready to dismiss the unfavorable ones.
  • Plan both branches in advance: what you'll do if you must open, and what you'll do if you're making the first counter-offer (control anchoring and counter-anchoring).

Anchoring: the evidence

  • Insufficient adjustment — people estimating 1×2×…×8 give far lower answers than people estimating 8×7×…×1: the first numbers seen anchor the estimate.
  • Even ignorant anchors bite — an engineer-salary estimate moves dramatically depending on whether an admittedly clueless source suggested $90K or $200K first.
  • Experts aren't immune — practicing Big Eight auditors asked whether management fraud exceeds 10-in-1000 vs. 200-in-1000 then estimated ~16.5 vs. ~43 per 1000. Same expertise, same question, wildly different answers.
  • The decoy effect — The Economist's famous pricing: with a $125 print-only option nobody chose sitting next to $125 print+web, 84% of MIT students picked the bundle; remove the decoy and only 32% did. Options no one selects still shape what people select.

Distributive vs. integrative

DistributiveIntegrative
Fixed pie — win/loseExpandable pie — win/win
Single issueMultiple issues
PositionsInterests
Claiming valueCreating and claiming value
CompetitiveCooperative problem-solving

Claiming-value playbook: define your BATNA → bottom line → optimistic-but-realistic aspiration → estimate their BATNA/RP/aspiration → open aggressively → never reveal your RP → concede only reciprocally. Creating-value playbook: same foundation, then analyze both parties' interests and construct multi-issue proposals that exploit differences in priorities. Be cooperative; be creative.

The Fisher, Ury & Patton guidelines tie it together: separate the people from the problem, focus on interests not positions, invent options for mutual gain, insist on objective criteria. And when impasses happen anyway, the usual culprits are emotions, escalation, unrealistic aspirations, a genuinely empty zone — or a fixed-pie assumption nobody questioned.

Texoil: when the bargaining zone looks empty

A gas-station sale where, on price alone, the two sides appear to have no overlap — the seller needs more than the buyer can pay. The lesson: an apparently negative ZOPA on a single issue is an invitation to expand the issue set. Asking about underlying interests (why do they need that number? what do they need it for?) can reveal non-price terms that bridge the gap. Pairs who treat it as a pure price haggle deadlock; pairs who explore interests find a deal. The professor's debrief points: there's no "right" answer in a negotiation, only a range of possible settlements; define (and improve) your BATNA before entering; negotiations are usually multi-dimensional, not uni-dimensional; and nearly every negotiation contains both distributive and integrative elements.

Positions vs. interests: a position is the number someone states; an interest is the need behind it. Price deadlocks often dissolve when you negotiate over interests.

Perception is constructed

The Müller-Lyer lines and nine-dots exercises made the framing point experientially: two identical lines look unequal, and a puzzle stays impossible until you let yourself draw outside the implied box. What you perceive in a negotiation — including the boundaries of what's negotiable — is a construction, and it can be reframed.

Cialdini's six principles of persuasion (assigned reading: "The Science of Persuasion")

Cialdini's argument: these six tendencies evolved because they usually serve us well, which is exactly why they work on us even when we see them coming. Each comes with decades of field evidence.

  • Reciprocation — people repay what they receive, even unsolicited gifts: adding free address labels to a charity mailing nearly doubled donation rates (18% → 35%). Crucially, it covers concessions too: in the rejection-then-retreat study, asking for a huge favor first and then "retreating" to a small one nearly tripled compliance (17% → 50%) vs. asking for the small favor alone. In negotiation terms: your concession is a gift that demands one back — which is why unilateral concessions that aren't framed as concessions buy you nothing.
  • Consistency — small commitments steer future behavior. A restaurant cut no-shows from 30% to 10% by changing "Please call if you have to change your plans" to "Will you please call…?" and pausing for a yes. Getting someone to state a position out loud, even trivially, makes them act consistently with it later.
  • Social validation — we decide what's correct by watching what similar others do. One planted man staring at the sky got 4% of passersby to look up; fifteen got 40%. The under-appreciated flip side: publicizing how common a bad behavior is normalizes it — "everyone's doing it" campaigns boomerang. Frame the desired behavior as the norm, not the problem as widespread.
  • Liking — we say yes to people we like, and liking is manufacturable: attractiveness (heart-association donations 42% vs. 23%), similarity ("I'm a student, too" doubled contributions), compliments (which worked in studies even when untrue), and cooperation (the car-sales manager cast as the "villain" so the salesperson can fight on your behalf).
  • Authority — symbols of expertise trigger deference whether or not the expertise is real: a suit and tie increased jaywalking-followers by 350%, and a TV actor who played a doctor sold medical advice effectively. Real authority is worth listening to; the skill is noticing when you're responding to the aura instead of the credentials.
  • Scarcity — things become more desirable as they become less available (cafeteria food ratings jumped when a fire made it temporarily unavailable). It applies to information too: telling customers about an upcoming beef shortage doubled orders, and telling them the shortage news came from exclusive sources multiplied them six-fold — scarce information about scarcity.
Culture reweights the six: in a four-country study of the same bank, employees decided whether to help a colleague by asking different questions — U.S.: "what has this person done for me?" (reciprocity); China: "is the requester tied to a senior person in my unit?" (authority); Spain: "is this a friend of my friends?" (liking); Germany: "do the rules say I should?" (consistency). Same principles everywhere, different weights.
The defense, per Cialdini: you can't switch these tendencies off, but you can name them in the moment. The test is whether the influence is honest — real scarcity, real expertise, a real favor — or smuggled in. Aim to be informed into saying yes, never tricked into it; and when persuading, use the principles only where they're genuinely true.
Session 3 · Sun Aug 23 (morning)

Distributive and integrative bargaining

Simulation: Leckenby · Reading: BFA ch. 5–6

Leckenby: negotiation stripped to pure signaling

A union-management wage negotiation with radical rules: simultaneous bids on cards, no talking, management's bids can only rise and the union's only fall, settle at the midpoint when they cross, and strike penalties that escalate daily (and hit management roughly twice as hard). With words banned, every bid becomes the entire message — which sets up the session's communication model: what's sent is not what's received, perceived, or interpreted. Your concession pattern is speaking a language, whether you planned the sentence or not.

The debrief: aspects of exchange

  • Leverage — who actually has the power? The side with less to lose from no deal. In Leckenby the union's flatter strike-cost curve gives it staying power despite management's endgame advantage.
  • Reciprocity and tit-for-tat — concessions are exchanges. Make them bilateral, never unilateral; a one-sided concession is read as information, not generosity.
  • Concession craft — leave yourself room (never "first and final"); make concessions smaller as you approach your goal (the pattern itself signals your limit); support every position with objective rationale; don't reflexively split the difference — the midpoint depends entirely on where the endpoints were planted; and remember people value process: a counterpart who fought for the deal accepts it more readily than one handed the same number.

Framing and prospect theory

The classic experiments, run live on the class: a layoff plan framed as saving 2,000 of 6,000 jobs gets chosen overwhelmingly over a gamble, while the mathematically identical plan framed as losing 4,000 jobs flips the room to the gamble. People are risk-averse in gains, risk-seeking in losses — so how you frame an offer (what the reference point is) changes what the other side will accept. Corollaries demonstrated:

  • Mental accounting — losing $120 in tickets makes people refuse to rebuy; losing $120 in cash doesn't stop them buying tickets. Same wealth change, different mental ledger.
  • Sunk costs — people brave a snowstorm for a $100 ticket they paid for but skip the same game on a free ticket. The $100 is gone either way.
  • Endowment effect — the minimum people demand to sell a painting they own far exceeds what they'd pay for an identical one. Owning something inflates its price — which is half of why sellers and buyers disagree so reliably.

Strikes in the news

Three real-world anchors for what strike costs look like outside the classroom: the 2024 ILA port strike (two days shut East and Gulf Coast ports; settled around a 62% wage increase over six years, with the White House pushing both sides); the 2023 Hollywood writers' strike (nearly five months over pay and streaming residuals; the 2007–08 predecessor ran 100 days); and the 1886 Haymarket affair, where the fight for the eight-hour day turned deadly. Same structure as Leckenby's cost table — escalating mutual damage with asymmetric pain — at historical scale.

The five bargaining styles

Follow-up to the pre-course questionnaire (Shell's bargaining styles assessment). The five approaches map onto two axes — how much the stakes matter vs. how much the relationship matters:

StyleStrengthsWeaknesses
CompetingProtects your interests; takes responsibilityReduces trust, hampers creativity, minimizes value creation
CollaboratingMaximizes joint value; improves relationshipsConsumes time; can weaken value-claiming; risks exploitation
CompromisingFast; meets everyone partway; equalizes powerInvites overstated positions; restricts value creation
AvoidingReduces tension; buys time for reflectionDelays resolution; can escalate the conflict
AccommodatingBuilds goodwill and relationshipsErases your influence; reads as weakness

The point isn't to have one style — it's that no style is right in every situation. Match the approach to your relational, substantive, and temporal goals, know your default, and deliberately practice the styles that don't come naturally.

Session 4 · Sun Aug 23 (afternoon)

Integrative bargaining

Simulation: Working Moms · Reading: BFA ch. 7–8

Working Moms: the anatomy of creating value

A TV-syndication sale — a studio rep selling a hit show to a local station — with three issues (price per title, runs per title, payment timing) plus an optional side deal on a filler show. Both sides hold outside options of similar size, so the interesting question isn't whether a deal exists; it's how big the parties can make the pie before dividing it. Total surplus = the gap between the two reservation points; integrative bargaining enlarges it, distributive bargaining divides it, and every real negotiation is both at once.

Three engines of joint gain, all "trading on differences"

  • Different marginal values (runs) — each additional broadcast run is worth more to the buyer (ad revenue) than it costs the seller (franchise dilution). Even in the range where extra runs actively hurt the seller, the buyer's gain is bigger — so moving up the runs scale and adjusting price to share the gain leaves both sides better off. A pair that "compromised" at the seller's favorite runs number destroyed value without noticing.
  • Different time preferences (payment timing) — the seller discounts future cash harder than the buyer does, so every delayed dollar costs the seller more than it saves the buyer. Joint gain comes from moving cash upfront and compensating the buyer with a lower price. The general form: whoever bears a cost more cheaply should bear it, with the price adjusting.
  • Different forecasts (contingent contracts) — seller believes in the show, buyer doesn't. Instead of arguing, bet on it: a refund if ratings disappoint, a surcharge if they exceed. Because each side computes the expected value with its own beliefs, the same bet has positive expected value for both simultaneously. Bigger bets have bigger expected value to both — bounded by how much risk each side can stomach and how much the bet distorts incentives.

Why contingent contracts earn their place

Beyond monetizing disagreement, contingent terms diagnose honesty (a bluffer won't take the bet), share risk, create performance incentives, counteract overconfidence, and solve trust problems when one side knows more than the other. The slogan version: don't argue over the future — bet on it.

The Full Yard lesson: not every issue belongs in the deal

The side deal on the filler show turned out to have a negative bargaining zone — the seller's floor (a standing outside offer) sat above the buyer's ceiling (a cheaper alternative filler). The correct outcome was no side deal at all, and pairs who forced one just transferred value from whoever blinked. Adding issues creates value only when a zone actually exists; the discipline is checking, not assuming.

Tactics for finding the trades

  • Don't assume a fixed pie — the other side differs from you in tastes, cost structure, time preference, risk appetite, and forecasts, and every difference is tradeable.
  • Negotiate multi-issue packages, never one issue at a time — sequential single-issue bargaining forecloses the trades. Withhold final approval until everything's on the table, then keep looking for improvements (post-settlement settlements).
  • Convey your own trade-offs in exchange rates ("delaying payments hurts me; it'd take ~30% more per title to work"), and probe theirs with trial balloons: "what if…" packages and either-or comparisons ("$65K at 8 runs, or $75K at 10 — which way do you lean?") reveal preferences without either side opening its books.
  • Insist on reciprocity in information as well as concessions — share a preference, ask for one back.
  • Fair ≠ integrative ≠ distributive: the class exercise contrasted outcomes that split evenly, outcomes that maximized one side, and outcomes that maximized the total — knowing which one you're optimizing for is half the argument.
