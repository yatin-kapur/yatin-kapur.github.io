Key takeaways (through Class 8)

The whole first month in one screen — the spine is A = L + E → dual effect → accrual timing → adjust at period-end.

  1. The accounting equation always holds. Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders' Equity. Every transaction touches at least two accounts (dual effect), and the equation stays in balance after every one.
  2. Four statements, two time frames. The balance sheet is a snapshot on a day; the income statement, statement of stockholders' equity, and statement of cash flows describe a period. Footnotes are an integral part of the statements.
  3. The statements articulate. Net income flows into retained earnings (via the SSE); ending cash on the cash flow statement is the cash on the ending balance sheet; ending equity lands on the balance sheet.
  4. Debit = left, credit = right — nothing more. Assets sit on the left of the equation, so debits increase them; liabilities and equity sit on the right, so credits increase those. Revenues increase with credits, expenses increase with debits (both through retained earnings).
  5. No entry for exchanges of promises. Signing a contract or hiring a president is not a transaction until goods, services, or cash change hands.
  6. Accrual accounting is the whole game. Revenue is recorded when earned (transfer of control), expenses when incurred to generate that revenue (matching) — regardless of when cash moves. Cash-basis and accrual-basis income can differ wildly on the same facts.
  7. Revenue recognition = the five-step model. Contract → performance obligations → transaction price → allocate → recognize when (or as) each obligation is satisfied.
  8. Cash timing creates four adjustment types. Cash before: deferred (prepaid) expense, deferred (unearned) revenue. Cash after: accrued expense, accrued revenue. Every adjusting entry moves one of these into the right period — and never touches Cash.
  9. Contra accounts preserve history. Depreciation accumulates in Accumulated Depreciation (a credit-balance contra-asset) so the balance sheet shows both cost and net book value = cost − accumulated depreciation.
  10. Temporary accounts close to retained earnings. Revenues, expenses, and dividends zero out at period-end so the next period starts fresh; balance sheet accounts carry forward.
  11. Adjustments are where discretion lives. Estimates (useful lives, accruals) require judgment, managers know the target, and the distribution of analyst forecast errors shows firms "meet or just beat" — earnings quality is a management choice.
  12. Ratios so far: current ratio = current assets / current liabilities (liquidity cushion; too high can mean inefficiency); margins = any income statement line / net sales (gross, operating, net — each isolates one layer of cost, and which layer leaks is the diagnosis); EPS = net income / average shares outstanding; quality of income = CFO / NI (how much profit is backed by cash).
  13. The cash flow statement is accrual accounting run in reverse. Indirect method: start from net income, add back noncash charges (depreciation), back out investing items (gains/losses), then undo every working-capital timing wedge — asset up, cash down; liability up, cash up. The three sections' signs tell a firm's life-cycle story (−/−/+ startup → +/−/− mature).
  14. Assets get estimated down, through contra-accounts, in the period of the related sale. The allowance for doubtful accounts is accumulated depreciation's sibling: bad debt expense hits income when the sale is recorded (matching + conservatism), and the later write-off is a wash — no income effect, no net-receivables effect. Percentage-of-sales estimates the expense directly; aging estimates the ending allowance and plugs the expense.
  15. Liabilities split by maturity and by measurement. Current liabilities (due ≤ 1 year) sit on the balance sheet at nominal amounts; long-term liabilities at present value — a dollar owed later is a smaller liability today. Contingent liabilities follow a probability × estimability grid: probable + estimable → record; probable-but-unestimable or reasonably-possible → footnotes; remote → ignore.
bookkeeping vocab

The machinery terms, in the order the accounting cycle uses them. On wide screens this stays pinned on the right while you scroll.

the recording machinery
account
Standardized format accumulating the dollar effect of transactions on one statement item (Cash, A/P, …).
chart of accounts
The company's list of all account titles, ordered assets → liabilities → equity → revenues → expenses.
journal entry
One transaction's dual effect in debits-equal-credits format; debits listed first, credits indented below.
compound entry
A journal entry touching more than two accounts (e.g., buy equipment with cash + a note).
general journal
Chronological log of all journal entries — the diary.
posting
Transferring journal-entry amounts into each account's ledger record.
general ledger
The record of every account's effects and running balance — the filing cabinet the journal feeds.
T-account
Ledger account shorthand: debits on the left, credits on the right, balance on the normal-balance side.
normal balance
The side an account increases on — debit for assets/expenses, credit for liabilities/equity/revenues.
period-end
trial balance
List of every account and its balance in two columns, proving total debits = total credits. Catches arithmetic errors only — not wrong-account postings or omitted transactions.
adjusting entries (AJEs)
End-of-period entries recording implicit transactions; always ≥1 income-statement + ≥1 balance-sheet account, never Cash.
adjusted trial balance
Trial balance re-run after AJEs — the version the statements are built from.
closing entries
Zero out temporary accounts (revenues, expenses, dividends) into Retained Earnings at period-end.
post-closing trial balance
Final check: debits = credits and all temporary accounts read zero.
temporary vs. permanent
Temporary = income statement accounts + dividends (reset each period). Permanent = balance sheet accounts (carry forward).
contra-account
Offset account glued to a parent with the opposite balance — Accumulated Depreciation (vs. PPE), Treasury Stock (vs. equity).
revenue & expense timing
cash basis
Record revenue when cash arrives, expense when cash leaves. Not GAAP.
accrual accounting
Record revenue when earned, expense when incurred, regardless of cash timing. Required by GAAP.
revenue recognition
Recognize when control of goods/services transfers to the customer, at the amount expected (five-step model).
matching principle
Expenses go in the same period as the revenues they helped generate.
deferral
Cash first, income statement later — prepaid expense (asset), unearned revenue (liability).
accrual (the entry type)
Income statement first, cash later — accrued revenue (receivable), accrued expense (payable).
unearned revenue
Liability for cash collected before delivery (deposits, gift cards, subscriptions).
prepaid expense
Asset for cash paid before consumption (rent, insurance, supplies).
the accounting cycle
during the period
1. analyze transactions → 2. record journal entries → 3. post to ledger.
at period-end
4. unadjusted trial balance → 5. adjusting entries → 6. adjusted trial balance → 7. statements (I/S → SSE → B/S, in that order) → 8. close.

The four financial statements · Ch. 1 · Class 1 (Jul 1)

Accounting is "the process of identifying, measuring and communicating financial information about an entity to permit informed judgements and decisions by users of the information." Financial accounting is the communicating half, aimed at external users — and the key insight about users: each one (investor, creditor, analyst, regulator, competitor, tax authority) will have a different motive, objective, and bias for looking at the same numbers.

Why it matters — accounting scandals are a global phenomenon. WorldCom hid falling profitability by capitalizing expenses (recording ~$3B of 2001 expenses as investments), reporting a $1.4B profit instead of a net loss; it went bankrupt a month after the auditor's discovery. Livedoor's CEO testified "I never studied accounting… a management book I read said to leave that to specialists" — and went to jail. The recurring lesson: managers can't outsource understanding the numbers, and neither can users.

Footnotes are an integral part of the financial statements — the numbers alone are never the whole story.

The fundamental accounting equation (investments = financing)

\[ \text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Stockholders' Equity} \]

Think of buying a house: the house (asset) is paid for with a mortgage (liability) plus your down payment (equity). A company is the same picture at scale — everything it owns was financed by someone, either creditors or owners. That's why the equation can never break: you can't own something nobody paid for.

Assets are the economic resources; liabilities are the creditors' claims on them; equity is whatever claim is left over for the owners after creditors are satisfied (which is why equity is defined as "residual interest": E = A − L).

Every transaction preserves the equality because it always touches two sides in an offsetting way — the "dual effect" that makes double-entry bookkeeping self-checking.

Expand equity into its components. Equity comes from two sources: capital paid in by owners and profits kept in the business:

\[ A = L + (\text{Paid-in Capital} + \text{Retained Earnings}^{*}) \]

Retained earnings is cumulative income never paid out (* = accumulated over all years):

\[ \text{Retained Earnings}^{*} = \text{Net Income}^{*} - \text{Dividends}^{*} \]

And net income is revenues minus expenses, so the fully expanded equation is:

\[ A = L + \text{Paid-in Capital} + \text{Rev}^{*} - \text{Exp}^{*} - \text{Div}^{*} \]

This expansion is the entire logic of the course: the income statement is a magnifying glass on one term of the balance sheet equation, and the statement of stockholders' equity is the bridge between them. It's also where debit/credit rules for revenues and expenses come from (Class 3).

Income statement equation

\[ \text{Revenues} - \text{Expenses} = \text{Net Income (or Loss)} \]

Revenues are increases in assets from delivering goods or services to customers; expenses are the costs incurred to generate those revenues. Net income is what's left — the period's scorecard. It is not cash: a sale on credit is revenue today, cash later.

Retained earnings roll-forward (the statement of stockholders' equity)

\[ RE_{\text{end}} = RE_{\text{beg}} + \text{Net Income} - \text{Dividends} \]

Retained earnings is a savings account the business keeps for its owners: each period's profit gets deposited, each dividend is a withdrawal. It accumulates over the company's whole life — which is why a young profitable company that pays no dividends builds retained earnings fast, and why RE is not a pile of cash (the profits are usually reinvested in inventory, equipment, etc.).

Statement of cash flows structure

\[ \Delta \text{Cash} = CFO + CFI + CFF \]

Every dollar that moved is sorted into three buckets by why it moved. Beginning cash plus the three buckets equals ending cash on the balance sheet.

CFO — cash from operating activities. The cash aspects of the day-to-day business: essentially a cash-basis income statement. Inflows: collections from customers, interest received. Outflows: payments to suppliers, salaries, interest paid, taxes. This is the engine — a healthy company generates positive CFO reliably, because it's the only bucket that refills itself.

CFI — cash from investing activities. The cash aspects of transactions in long-term assets used to produce goods and services. Outflows: buying plant and equipment, buying securities or whole companies, lending money to others. Inflows: selling PP&E or investments, collecting principal on loans made. Negative CFI is usually a good sign for a growing firm — it means money is being planted, not that it's being lost.

CFF — cash from financing activities. The cash aspects of transactions with the firm's funders — debt and equity. Inflows: issuing stock, borrowing from banks or bondholders. Outflows: repaying principal, buying back stock, paying dividends. (Note the asymmetry: interest paid is CFO under US GAAP, but the principal repayment is CFF.)

Read as a pattern, the signs tell a life story: a startup is (−CFO, −CFI, +CFF) — burning cash, investing, living on funders. A healthy mature firm is (+CFO, −CFI, −CFF) — operations fund the investing and return the surplus. A firm with (−CFO, +CFI, +CFF) is selling the furniture and borrowing to cover a business that doesn't work.

Why anyone cares, given we already have net income: to see whether reported profits are actually realized as cash (the Enron question), to understand the sources of cash in detail, to forecast future cash flows, to feed DCF valuation, and to judge liquidity (lenders and rating agencies). Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact.

beyond the slides — what the textbook adds (ch. 1)

Le-Nature's Inc. — the chapter's framing fraud. Reported $275M of sales; real sales were ~$32M, liabilities understated by $200M, new loans repaying old ones (a Ponzi). CEO got 20 years; the auditors paid $50M, Wachovia $80M. All three safeguards failed at once: no internal controls, duped auditors, a crony board. Statement mechanics SmartBook likes: every statement heading has four parts — entity, statement title, date ("At Dec 31") or period ("For the Year Ended"), and unit of measure. Assets are listed by ease of conversion to cash, liabilities by maturity. Accounts payable = credit purchases without a written contract; notes payable = borrowing with one. And dividends are a distribution, never an expense.

Conceptual framework (classic quiz): fundamental qualities = relevance (predictive/feedback value, material) and faithful representation (complete, neutral, free from error); enhancing qualities = comparability, verifiability, timeliness, understandability. GAAP ≠ tax rules — companies keep a separate set for the IRS. Foreign firms listed in the US may report under IFRS.

Business entities (Supplement A): sole proprietorship and partnership are legally inseparable from owners (general partners have unlimited liability) but are separate accounting entities; corporations give limited liability, continuity, transferable ownership, and capital-raising power at the cost of double taxation.

New key terms: accounting entity (the organization the data describes), accounting period (the span the statements cover).

Balance sheet & transaction analysis · Ch. 2 · Class 2 (Jul 6)

The three definitions (worth memorizing verbatim)

  • Asset: "a resource controlled/owned by an entity as a result of past events/transactions and from which future economic benefits are expected to flow to the firm."
  • Liability: "a probable future sacrifice/transfer of economic benefits arising from present obligations of the entity as a result of past events/transactions."
  • Stockholders' equity: "the residual interest in (or remaining claims against) the firm's assets after deducting liabilities" — A − L = E.

Recognition (getting onto the balance sheet at all) requires three things: measurability (a relevant attribute measurable with sufficient reliability), reliability (representationally faithful, verifiable, neutral), and relevance (capable of making a difference to decisions). There's a natural trade-off between reliability and relevance — which is why genuinely valuable things like employees, internally built brands, and know-how never appear as assets: relevant, but not reliably measurable. It's also why LVMH could pay $16.2B for Tiffany when Tiffany's market cap was $10.6B and its book equity far less — market value ≠ book value.

Classification: current assets convert to cash (or get used) within a year or one operating cycle; current liabilities are due within a year. The classified balance sheet lists current assets first in order of liquidity, then long-term (tangible vs. intangible, financial vs. non-financial); current liabilities before long-term; equity last, split into common stock (shares × par value), additional paid-in capital (issue price above par), and retained earnings.

Debits and credits

Debit means left. Credit means right. That's the entire content of the words — no moral valence. The direction of "increase" follows from which side of the accounting equation an account lives on:

The handy trick: assets are on the left side of A = L + E, so the left side of the T-account (debit) increases them. Liabilities and equity are on the right side of the equation, so the right side (credit) increases them. One sentence replaces a page of memorization.

Every transaction is analyzed in two steps: (1) what was received and what was given — identify the accounts, classify each as A/L/E, determine direction; (2) verify A = L + E still balances and total debits = total credits. Only four patterns exist for pure balance-sheet transactions:

TypePatternExample
IAsset ↑ and Liability/Equity ↑Borrow cash; issue stock
IIAsset ↓ and Liability/Equity ↓Repay a loan
IIIOne asset ↑, another asset ↓ (swap)Buy equipment for cash
IVOne liability/equity ↓, another ↑ (swap)Refinance a payable with a note

The bookkeeping pipeline: transactions → documentation → journal entries → post to the general ledger (T-accounts) → trial balance → financial statements. A journal entry records the dual effect at a point in time; the T-account accumulates a running balance per account.

No entry for exchanges of promises. Signing a $10,000 website contract: no entry. Hiring a president at $85,000/year starting next January: no entry. Ordering supplies: no entry until they arrive. An accounting transaction requires that goods, services, or cash actually change hands — this shows up on every exam.

Current ratio

\[ \text{Current Ratio} = \frac{\text{Current Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}} \]

If everything due in the next year came due at once, could you cover it with what turns into cash in the next year? Above 1 means yes with cushion; higher means more cushion — but a very high ratio can signal inefficiency (resources parked in idle cash or slow inventory rather than at work).

Class examples: Tiffany at 5.24 (enormous cushion, mostly diamond inventory — characteristic of luxury jewelry, but arguably inefficient) vs. Delta at ~0.76 (fine for an airline, because big current liabilities like air-traffic liability and loyalty deferred revenue are settled by flying people, not paying cash). Same ratio, opposite stories — always interpret against the business model. Competitor spread: Swatch 5.74, Burberry 1.69, Prada 1.3, Pandora 0.62.

beyond the slides — what the textbook adds (ch. 2)

Four assumptions/principles (the chapter's core additions): separate entity (the business's books exclude the owner's personal transactions — an owner borrowing for personal use = no entry); going concern (assume the business continues; if bankruptcy looms, assets get valued at liquidation instead); monetary unit (report in the national currency, with no adjustment for inflation); historical cost (record at cash-equivalent value on the transaction date, including the value of any noncash consideration like a trade-in).

Transactions come in two types: external (exchanges with other parties) and internal events (measurable but exchange-free — using up prepaid insurance, wearing out equipment). Promises-for-promises still don't count, but big commitments (long-term employment contracts) may need note disclosure. Account-naming rules worth knowing cold: "payable" → always a liability, "receivable" → always an asset, "unearned" → always a liability, "prepaid" → always an asset.

What's not on the balance sheet: internally developed intangibles (Meta's balance sheet shows no Facebook trademark; Coca-Cola shows no Coke-formula asset but does carry $14.2B of purchased trademarks — purchased vs. internally developed is the line). Off-balance-sheet obligations hide in the "Commitments and Contingencies" note — read the notes.

Exam-style skill the book calls out: inferring activity from a T-account — beg + increases − decreases = end, solve for the unknown (A/P: 600 + 1,500 purchases − paid = 300 → paid 1,800). Also: par value has no relationship to market price (it's a legal amount from the charter), and treasury stock — repurchased shares — reduces equity.

Chipotle color: all 3,400+ stores company-owned (no franchising, unlike McDonald's at 95% franchised); its unearned revenue = unredeemed gift cards; McDonald's turned a $360M investment in it into ~$1.4B. Current ratio 1.57, with the same too-high-can-mean-inefficient caveat as the slides' Tiffany example.

New: the identity is unchanged, but every term now has anatomy — assets and liabilities split current vs. non-current (the current ratio compares the two current buckets), and paid-in capital splits into common stock at par plus additional paid-in capital. Income statement & accrual accounting · Ch. 3 · Classes 2–3 (Jul 6–8)

The operating (cash) cycle: buy or make product on credit → pay suppliers → deliver to customers on credit → collect from customers → repeat. Accrual accounting exists because the interesting events (earning, consuming) and the cash events (collecting, paying) happen at different points on this loop.

  • Revenues: "increases in assets or settlement of liabilities in exchange for delivery of goods or services." They increase retained earnings.
  • Expenses: "decreases in assets or increases in liabilities in the process of delivering goods or services." They decrease retained earnings.
  • Gains/losses: same balance-sheet impact, but from peripheral transactions (selling the old delivery truck, not selling coffee) — kept separate so users can see core operations cleanly.

Cash basis records revenue when cash arrives and expense when cash leaves. Accrual accounting — required by GAAP — records "revenues when earned and expenses when incurred, regardless of the timing of cash receipts or payments."

Revenue recognition: the five-step model

The converged GAAP/IFRS standard, one sentence: revenue recognition depicts transfer of control to the customer in an amount reflecting the consideration the entity expects to be entitled to, applied to products, services, and licenses.

  1. Identify the contract — do we have a deal?
  2. Identify the performance obligations — who is doing what?
  3. Determine the transaction price — what do we expect to be owed?
  4. Allocate the price across the obligations — split it if there are several.
  5. Recognize revenue when (or as) each obligation is satisfied — i.e., when control transfers.

Amazon's 10-K is the class case study, because one company runs nearly every recognition pattern at once: retail revenue when control transfers (delivery to carrier, or to the customer for Amazon-delivered orders); third-party seller commissions when the service is rendered; Prime as a single "stand-ready obligation" recognized ratably over the subscription period; advertising by clicks/impressions delivered; AWS as the customer consumes usage. Same five steps, five different answers.

Expense matching principle: "costs incurred to generate revenues should be recognized in the same period, regardless of when cash is paid." Inventory sits as an asset until sold (then becomes cost of sales); a building becomes depreciation expense over decades of use.

Margins: reading the income statement as percentages of sales

A multi-step income statement subtotals its way down from sales, and each subtotal isolates one layer of cost:

Sales (net) − Cost of goods sold → Gross profit (product economics) − Operating expenses (SG&A, etc.) → Operating income (running the business) ± Interest, other income → Pretax income (+ financing choices) − Income taxes → Net income (bottom line)

The general margin recipe — any income statement line ÷ net sales

\[ \text{X Margin} = \frac{X}{\text{Net Sales}} \quad \text{for } X \in \{\text{Gross Profit, Operating Income, Pretax Income, Net Income, …}\} \]

Every margin answers the same question at a different altitude: of each dollar of sales, how many cents are still alive at this line? Gross margin is the economics of the product itself (price vs. direct cost). Operating margin adds the cost of actually running the business — stores, salaries, marketing. Net profit margin is after everything, including financing and taxes — the compression ratio of the whole company.

The layering is what makes them diagnostic. If gross margin is stable but operating margin is falling, the product is fine and the overhead is bloating. If gross margin itself is eroding, there's price competition or input cost pressure — no amount of SG&A discipline fixes that. And because net sales is the common denominator ("common-size" analysis), margins let you compare a $15B company to a $500M one, or the same company across years, on equal footing.

Two cautions from class: compare within an industry (grocery margins and software margins live on different planets — nature of the product and intensity of competition set the baseline), and expect well-run firms to hold or improve margins over time.

Adjusting entries, closing & earnings quality · Ch. 4 · Class 4 (Jul 13)

The accounting cycle: during the period, analyze → journalize → post. At period-end: unadjusted trial balance → adjusting entries → adjusted trial balance → financial statements → close. Adjustments exist because some transactions are implicit — no invoice or cash movement flags them; they happen through the passage of time (rent expiring, subscriptions being earned, interest accruing, equipment wearing out). Adjusting entries get revenues into the period earned and expenses into the period incurred so the statements are true and fair.

Golden rule: never debit or credit Cash in an adjusting entry. If cash moved, it was an explicit transaction recorded when it happened. Adjustments only re-time revenues and expenses. (Repeated twice in the deck; will be on the exam.)

Depreciation & contra-assets

Straight-line depreciation and net book value

\[ \text{Depreciation}/\text{yr} = \frac{\text{Cost} - \text{Residual Value}}{\text{Useful Life}} \qquad \text{NBV} = \text{Cost} - \text{Accumulated Depreciation} \]

A $16,500 desk set used for 5 years isn't a $16,500 expense in year one — it helps generate revenue for five years, so matching spreads its cost: $3,300/year, $275/month. Depreciation is cost allocation, not valuation — no one claims the furniture is worth exactly NBV.

The credit doesn't hit Furniture directly; it accumulates in Accumulated Depreciation, a contra-asset (credit-balance account glued to its parent). That preserves the original cost on the balance sheet: readers see both what was paid and how much has been consumed. Delta's balance sheet shows the scale: $18.7B of accumulated depreciation against flight equipment with estimated lives of 20–34 years — and every one of those life estimates is management judgment.

Interest accrual

\[ \text{Interest} = \text{Principal} \times \text{Annual Rate} \times \text{Fraction of Year} \]

Interest is rent paid for the use of money, and it accrues with time whether or not a payment date has arrived. Borrow $10,000 at 10%: each month you owe another $83.33 (10,000 × 0.10 × 1/12), so each month-end brings Dr Interest Expense 83.33 / Cr Interest Payable 83.33. Stated rates are annual unless said otherwise — the fraction-of-year term is where points are lost.

Closing the books

Permanent accounts (balance sheet) carry forward. Temporary accounts (revenues, expenses, gains, losses, dividends) measure one period's performance, so at period-end they're zeroed into retained earnings: debit each revenue for its balance, credit each expense, close dividends — and RE ends at beginning + NI − dividends, exactly matching the SSE. Next period starts from zero.

Earnings per share

\[ \text{EPS} = \frac{\text{Net Income} - \text{Preferred Dividends}}{\text{Avg. Common Shares Outstanding}} \]

Slices the period's profit into per-share pieces so it's comparable to the share price — the number analysts forecast and the bottom line of every income statement. Preferred dividends come out of the numerator because they're spoken for before common shareholders get anything.

Quality of earnings

Adjustments are the soft underbelly of the income statement, for two reasons: managers know where performance stands relative to analyst forecasts before the books close, and many adjustments rest on estimates requiring judgment (useful lives, accrual amounts). The evidence: plot analyst forecast errors (actual EPS − forecast) across thousands of firms and the histogram spikes just above zero — lots of small beats, suspiciously few small misses. Companies "meet or just beat." Amazon's earnings history in class showed surprises from +60.5% to −8.4% against consensus. Read reported earnings knowing the discretion embedded in them.

Land never depreciates. Before adjustment: deferrals leave an asset/liability overstated and the expense/revenue understated; accruals leave both sides understated.

Exam trap: Retained Earnings on the adjusted trial balance is stale — it still excludes the current period's net income until closing. The statements must be built in strict order (I/S → SSE → B/S) so NI can flow through; an income-statement error cascades into both other statements.

Closing extras: a post-closing trial balance verifies debits = credits and all temporary accounts read zero. Treasury stock is a contra-equity account (the book's second contra-account after accumulated depreciation). EPS is the only ratio GAAP requires on the face of the statements.

Earnings quality, quantified: analysts compute CFO ÷ NI — higher means earnings are backed by cash; a large gap (high income, low operating cash) flags aggressive accruals. The SEC's EPS Initiative literally screens filings for "meet or just beat" patterns.

Statement of cash flows · Ch. 12 · Class 5 (Jul 15)

The income statement tells you whether the products are economically viable; the cash flow statement tells you whether the same transactions actually generate cash. Three reasons it exists: cash confirms that reported profits are real (cash is hard to manipulate; accruals aren't), it shows which part of the business produces or consumes cash, and free cash flow is the raw input to valuation (DCF). Caveat from class: firms can still reclassify items between sections opportunistically.

Direct vs. indirect

Both methods produce identical CFO, CFI, CFF, and Δcash — they differ only in how the operating section is presented. The direct method lists gross receipts and payments (collections from customers, cash paid to suppliers…) — easier to read, much harder to prepare, preferred by the FASB, used by almost nobody. The indirect method reconciles net income to CFO — harder intuition, easy preparation, used by ~99% of large US companies. Depreciation never appears on a direct-method statement at all (it isn't a cash flow). CVS's 10-K shows both at once — direct section plus the required indirect reconciliation ($15.9B CFO in 2020, either way).

Indirect method — the template

Net income + Depreciation / depletion / amortization + Loss (− Gain) on disposal of long-term assets + Decrease in operating asset − Increase in operating asset + Increase in operating liability − Decrease in operating liability = Net cash flow from operating activities

Net income counted things that weren't cash, and missed cash that wasn't income — the template just un-counts and re-counts. Depreciation reduced income but no one paid anybody (the cash left years ago, when the asset was bought, in investing) — add it back. A gain on selling equipment sits inside net income, but the entire sale proceeds are reported in investing — subtract the gain or you'd count it twice (add back losses for the same reason).

The working-capital rules are all one idea: an operating asset growing means cash got trapped on the balance sheet (receivables up = sales you didn't collect; inventory up = purchases you haven't sold) — subtract. An operating liability growing means someone else is floating you (payables up = expenses you haven't paid) — add. Memorize it as: asset up, cash down; liability up, cash up.

Class Q&A: why start from NI at all? Precisely to display how income and cash differ — the reconciliation IS the information.

From the balance sheet identity, cash is what's left after everything else:

\[ \Delta \text{Cash} = \Delta L + \Delta SE - \Delta \text{Noncash Assets} \]

Split ΔSE into net income and dividends, and split every other account by which activity drives it (operating, investing, financing). Group the operating pieces:

\[ CFO = NI + \text{noncash charges} \pm \text{gains/losses} - \Delta \text{op. assets} + \Delta \text{op. liabilities} \]

— which is the template. The indirect method isn't a trick; it's the accounting equation differenced over a year and sorted into three buckets. That's also why the statement can be built from two balance sheets plus the income statement.

Every "cash collected/paid" number comes from the same pattern — beginning + ins − outs = ending, solve for the unknown:

Collections: Beg A/R + Sales − Collections = End A/R Purchases: Beg Inventory + Purchases − COGS = End Inventory Payments: Beg A/P + Purchases − Payments = End A/P Dividends: Beg RE + NI − Dividends declared = End RE

Shortcut forms: cash from customers = Sales − ΔA/R; payments to suppliers = COGS + ΔInventory − ΔA/P; payments for an expense = expense + Δprepaid − Δpayable. Same machinery works for investing (Beg PP&E + purchases − book value sold = End PP&E; sale proceeds = book value sold + gain − loss) and financing.

Free cash flow

\[ \text{FCF} = CFO - \text{CapEx} \qquad \text{(unlevered: } EBIT(1-t) + D\&A - \Delta WC - \text{CapEx)} \]

The cash left over after the business has paid to keep itself running and growing — free to fund expansion, retire debt, or go back to shareholders. It's the number DCF valuation discounts (at WACC for the whole firm; subtract interest and debt retirement to value just the equity). Definitions vary — the textbook's version also subtracts dividends.

Why not just use EBITDA as "cash flow"? Buffett's answer from class: "Does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?" EBITDA ignores the reinvestment a real business can't skip; FCF doesn't. The professor's mental image: cash flow is the tap the firm actually drinks from.

beyond the slides — what the textbook adds (ch. 12)

Cash means cash + cash equivalents — investments with original maturity ≤ 3 months, measured from when the holder bought it (a 3-year T-note purchased 3 months before maturity qualifies; one held from issuance never does). Moving money between cash and equivalents is not a cash flow at all.

Account-tagging fine print (SmartBook bait): short-term investments are investing, not operating current assets. Three current liabilities are financing: dividends payable, short-term bank debt, current maturities of long-term debt. Retained earnings gets tagged both O (the net-income part) and F (the dividends part). Asset-sale proceeds go to investing at the full cash received regardless of gain or loss — the gain/loss only ever appears as the operating back-out.

Two ratios: quality of income = CFO ÷ NI (National Beverage 1.15; Coca-Cola 1.08, PepsiCo 1.47) — four reasons it strays from 1.0: growth inflating receivables/inventory, seasonality, aggressive recognition, sloppy working-capital management. Capital acquisitions ratio = CFO ÷ cash paid for PP&E (NBC 7.41 vs Pepsi 2.44) — how much of capex operations fund internally; compare only within an industry. W. T. Grant went bankrupt in 1975 with healthy net income and collapsing CFO — the divergence is the classic fraud/decline early warning.

Required supplemental disclosures: significant noncash investing/financing transactions (buy a building with a seller mortgage, convert debt to stock) in a separate schedule, and — under the indirect method — cash paid for interest and for income taxes.

Format trivia: the FASB recommends the direct method; ~99% of large US companies use indirect anyway (cost). Under IFRS, interest paid may go in financing and interest/dividends received in investing — GAAP fixes all three in operating (dividends paid excepted: financing).

Receivables & bad debt · Ch. 6 · Class 6 (Jul 20)

A receivable's face value is a hope, not a fact — some customers will refuse or be unable to pay, and cash discounts and returns shave more off. So at each balance-sheet date the question becomes: how much of this will actually convert to cash? Two principles force the answer to arrive early: conservatism (don't overstate assets) and matching (the cost of uncollectible sales belongs in the period of the sale — not years later when the customer finally goes broke). The lecture covered only the bad-debt machinery; net sales components and the bank reconciliation live in the textbook block below.

The two accounts

  • Allowance for doubtful accounts — the manager's estimate of what will not be collected. A contra-asset with a normal credit balance, sitting under gross A/R exactly the way accumulated depreciation sits under PP&E. Neither gross A/R nor the allowance ever appears on the income statement.
  • Bad debt expense — the income-statement cost of uncollectibility, reported within SG&A. It's how the allowance gets filled.

Net receivables and the allowance roll-forward

\[ \text{Net A/R} = \text{Gross A/R} - \text{Allowance} \qquad \text{Allowance}_{beg} + \text{BDE} - \text{Write-offs} = \text{Allowance}_{end} \]

Net A/R is the number the balance sheet reports: management's honest guess at future cash. The allowance is a reservoir — bad debt expense pours estimate in; write-offs drain specific corpses out. The roll-forward identity is the workhorse of every exam problem: given any three pieces, solve for the fourth. In-class example (Target's card business): ending 430 + write-offs 414 − beginning 690 = bad debt expense 154 — inferred purely from the T-account.

Real-world reservoir sizes vary wildly with the customer base: Ford's allowance ran 0.55% of gross finance receivables (car buyers with credit checks), Target's card business ~6.6% — jumping to 11.1% in the 2008 crisis — and hospital operator HCA's allowance approached 94% of its uninsured-patient receivables. Same machinery, different worlds.

Receivables turnover & days sales outstanding

\[ \text{A/R Turnover} = \frac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Average Net A/R}} \qquad \text{DSO} = \frac{365}{\text{Turnover}} \]

How many times a year the receivables pool refills — and its inverse in days: how long a sale sits before becoming cash. Class example: sales 120,000 on average net A/R of 10,000 → turnover 12 → 30 days to collect. Falling turnover with growing sales = receivables piling up = the same early-warning wedge the cash flow statement exposes (an A/R increase is a CFO subtraction).

The comparability trap, via the class case: Target's fiscal-2007 DSO was 42.3 days vs. Walmart's 3.7 — not because Target collected slowly, but because Target owned its credit-card receivables and Walmart's card was run by a bank. Know what's inside the numerator's counterpart before comparing. (Epilogue: after the 2008 crisis pushed its allowance from 6.6% to 11.1% of gross receivables, Target sold the whole $5.9B card book to TD Bank in 2012 — its balance sheet now has no A/R line at all.)

beyond the slides — what the textbook adds (ch. 6)

Net sales (skipped in lecture, SmartBook still asks): Net sales = sales revenue − sales discounts − returns & allowances (both contra-revenues). Credit-card fees are not contra-revenue — they're SG&A expense. A "2/10, n/30" discount means 2% off if paid in 10 days, net due in 30 — and forgoing it costs an implied 37% annual rate (2/98 over 20 days, annualized), so customers should take it even on borrowed money. Trade discounts (off list price) are a different thing entirely — applied before anything is recorded.

FOB terms: shipping point — title passes at shipment, buyer owns goods in transit, seller books revenue at shipment; destination — title passes on delivery, seller owns in-transit goods. Decides both revenue timing and whose inventory the truck contains.

Bank reconciliation (also skipped in lecture): bank side gets timing items — + deposits in transit, − outstanding checks, ± bank errors; book side gets news from the statement — + interest earned and bank collections, − NSF checks and service charges, ± book errors. Both sides meet at true cash, which is what the balance sheet reports. Only book-side items get journal entries, and an NSF check becomes a receivable again (Dr A/R / Cr Cash). Internal control's spine: whoever handles cash never records it or reconciles the account.

Fine print worth points: estimation misses are corrected prospectively — next period's expense, no restatement (Skechers added $10.2M of COVID-era BDE in one quarter). The percentage method applies its rate to credit sales only — cash sales can't go bad. Turnover uses net sales because net credit sales are rarely disclosed. And Monsanto's rebate games (parking $56M of rebates in SG&A instead of against net sales) bought an $80M SEC penalty — misclassification within the income statement is still fraud.

The estimate flows through income in the sale's period (bad debt expense); the eventual write-off just relabels the loss inside the asset — a wash everywhere. This is the chapter where the balance sheet stops being a record of transactions and starts being a set of judgments — which is exactly why the allowance ratio (allowance ÷ gross A/R) and receivables turnover are earnings-quality dials, not just liquidity stats. Income statement so far: gains a standing estimated expense inside SG&A, sized by percentage-of-sales (expense-first) or aging (allowance-first).

Inventory & cost of goods sold · Ch. 7 · Class 7 (Jul 22)

Every sale of goods is two entries — revenue at retail, and the one this chapter cares about: Dr COGS / Cr Inventory at cost. The core issue is allocation and valuation: of everything spent on goods, how much stays on the balance sheet as an asset (inventory) and how much goes to the income statement (COGS)? Merchandisers buy finished goods (Gap, American Eagle); manufacturers build them through three inventory accounts — raw materials → work in process (materials + direct labor + overhead) → finished goods (Microsoft's note splits its $2,181M exactly that way).

What goes into inventory cost

Invoice price + freight-in + inspection + preparation − discounts and allowances. The class example: steel invoiced at $160,000 FOB shipping point, $10,000 freight (buyer's, so capitalized), $15,000 flaw allowance, 2% cash discount taken → 160,000 + 10,000 − 15,000 − 2,900 = $152,100. The trap inside: the 2% applies to the invoice net of the allowance (2% × 145,000), not the gross.

The COGS identity

\[ \text{Beg. Inventory} + \text{Purchases} = \underbrace{\text{Goods Available}}_{\text{the pool}} = \text{End. Inventory} + \text{COGS} \]

Everything that was ever in the store either is still there or was sold — the pool has exactly two exits. Given any three pieces, the fourth is a plug, and that's a whole genre of exam problem. The class fire-loss case (Liao Co.): inventory 65,000 + net purchases 185,000 + freight-in 20,000 = 270,000 available; sales of 200,000 at a 20% gross margin imply COGS = 160,000; so the merchandise destroyed = 270,000 − 160,000 = 110,000. The gross-margin percentage is how you conjure COGS when no count exists.

When identical units were bought at different prices, which cost leaves when one sells? Four GAAP answers, run in class on the same 25 units (Discount Computer: goods available $4,198; 17 sold, 8 left; purchases at $160→$162→$170→$176, i.e., rising prices):

Write-downs: lower of cost or market

Damaged or unwanted inventory gets written down to market — item by item, and only down, never up ("what if the value recovers? Oh well…"). The class example (Amanda Corp.): three items costing 35,000 total, only the one with market below cost gets touched → Dr COGS 400 / Cr Inventory 400, carrying value 34,600, which becomes the new permanent cost basis under GAAP (IFRS allows reversals via an allowance). Estée Lauder's 10-K shows the industrial version: an obsolescence reserve driven by age and forecast sales.

Inventory turnover

\[ \text{Inventory Turnover} = \frac{\text{COGS}}{\text{Average Inventory}} \qquad \text{Days to Sell} = \frac{365}{\text{Turnover}} \]

How fast the shelf clears. Note the numerator is COGS, not sales — inventory is carried at cost, so the comparison must be cost-to-cost (receivables turnover uses sales because A/R is carried at selling price; that asymmetry is a favorite MC distractor). Higher is generally better — less cash tied up, less storage and obsolescence risk — but too high courts stockouts. Holding inventory is a genuine trade-off: meeting demand and smoothing production versus holding cost, theft, obsolescence, and trapped cash flow.

state of the equation · after ch. 7 \[ \text{BI} + \text{Purchases} - \text{EI}_{\text{(FIFO? LIFO? avg?)}} = \text{COGS} \]

New: inventory's balance-sheet value is now a cost-flow assumption — the same physical goods produce four different asset values and four different incomes, legally. The pool identity welds the choice to the income statement: every dollar assigned to ending inventory is a dollar not in COGS, which is why the FIFO–LIFO gap shows up twice, once on each statement. Add LCM's one-way conservatism (write down, never up) and inventory joins receivables as an estimated asset — except here management picks the method, not just the number, and the tax bill moves with it.

Long-term assets · Ch. 8 · Class 7 (Jul 22)

Midterm II scope, straight from the slides: ROA, asset turnover, and fixed-asset turnover are marked "ignore for midterm 2." Double-declining balance and IFRS revaluation are FYI-only. What's squarely in: capitalization, straight-line and units-of-production depreciation, changes in estimates, disposals, intangibles/goodwill, impairment.

The framework slide for every long-lived asset: acquire → measure over time → change → dispose. Tangibles depreciate (except land — never), natural resources deplete, definite-life intangibles amortize, and indefinite-life intangibles (trademarks, goodwill) sit untouched but get impairment-tested annually. One rule towers over the section: only purchased intangibles go on the balance sheet — internally developed R&D, brands, and training are expensed immediately, which is why Starbucks' most valuable asset, its brand, appears nowhere on its $14.4B balance sheet.

Acquisition: what gets capitalized

Acquisition cost = purchase price + everything needed to make the asset ready for its intended use — sales tax, freight, legal fees, installation, pre-use repairs — but not financing charges. The in-class problem (IRS computer equipment): invoice 400,000 − 2% discount 8,000 + freight 4,400 + installation 8,000 + pre-use repairs 9,000 = 413,400. Deliberately excluded: the seller's commission, the purchasing manager's salary, and repairs after the asset entered service (those are expenses). Capitalize vs. expense in one line: extends life or capacity beyond a year → capitalize (roof replacement); keeps it running → expense (worn part, routine repair). WorldCom's $11B fraud was this one line item done backwards, and it bought Ebbers 25 years and the world Sarbanes-Oxley.

Depreciation: allocating the cost

The two examinable methods (DDB is FYI)

\[ \text{SL: } \frac{\text{Cost} - \text{Residual}}{\text{Useful Life}} \text{ per year} \qquad \text{UoP: } \frac{\text{Cost} - \text{Residual}}{\text{Total Est. Production}} \times \text{Actual Production} \]

Depreciation is cost allocation, not valuation — spreading (cost − salvage) over the periods the asset earns revenue. Straight-line fits assets that produce evenly through time; units-of-production fits assets that wear out by use (the class machine: $90,000 depreciable ÷ 5,000 units = $18/unit, so a 1,300-unit year costs $23,400). Both land on the same ending NBV = residual value; they just travel differently.

Three inputs, only one fact: cost is known; useful life and salvage are estimates — which makes depreciation a lever. Singapore Airlines stretched aircraft lives from 10 to 15 years in 2001 and saved $265M of expense per year, flattering a bad year's income entirely legally (disclosed in the notes). A fully depreciated asset that's still working just sits at its residual value — no more expense, no removal, foresight isn't perfect.

Changes in estimates are prospective — no restatement; the new facts apply to the book value going forward. The F-22 class example, three steps: (1) NBV after 3.5 years = 171M − 3.5 × 28.8M = 70.2M; (2) add the 16.7M capital improvement → new basis 86.9M; (3) depreciate (86.9 − new salvage 28) over the remaining life of 2.5 years → 23.56M/year. Basis, then salvage, then remaining life — in that order, every time.

Disposal: settle up with reality

First bring depreciation up to the disposal date, then remove both the asset (at original cost) and its accumulated depreciation, and let the plug be the answer: gain or loss = proceeds − net book value. The class triple (hotel furniture, cost 3,000,000, accumulated 2,750,000, NBV 250,000): sold at 250,000 → no gain/loss; at 800,000 → gain 550,000; at 200,000 → loss 50,000. Ch. 12 echo: the full proceeds land in investing, and the gain/loss gets backed out of operating.

Intangibles, goodwill, impairment

  • Goodwill exists only from buying a company: purchase price − fair value of net assets acquired ($100M paid for $80M of net assets → $20M goodwill). It's a residual, never amortized, impairment-tested annually — and it was the single biggest asset in Starbucks' acquisition example ($815.6M of $1,886.8M).
  • Definite-life intangibles (patents, copyrights, licenses) amortize straight-line over the shorter of economic and legal life — with no accumulated-amortization account; credit the asset directly. Leasehold improvements amortize over the shorter of their life and the lease term.
  • Impairment is a two-step: test — is NBV > undiscounted future cash flows? — and if so, measure: loss = NBV − fair value (Dr Impairment Loss / Cr Accumulated Depreciation). Goodwill now gets a one-step carrying-vs-fair-value test. Sears wrote its trade name down three years running ($381M, then $72M more) and filed Chapter 11 in 2018 — impairments are often the smoke before the fire.

Two derived metrics from the PepsiCo breakout worth knowing (they're inference skills, not banned ratios): average depreciable life ≈ gross depreciable assets ÷ annual depreciation expense (PepsiCo: ~15.6 years) and percent depreciated = accumulated depreciation ÷ gross depreciable assets (~61% — an aging asset base). Both exclude land and construction in progress, which don't depreciate.

state of the equation · after ch. 8 \[ \text{PP\&E}_{net} = \underbrace{\text{Capitalized cost}}_{\text{ready-for-use}} - \underbrace{\text{Acc. Depr.}}_{\text{life, salvage: estimates}} \qquad \text{Gain/Loss on disposal} = \text{Proceeds} - \text{NBV} \]

New: the operating-asset tour is complete — receivables (allowance), inventory (cost-flow assumption), and now long-term assets, where the judgment calls stack three deep: what to capitalize (the WorldCom line), how fast to allocate (life and salvage estimates — the Singapore Airlines lever), and when to admit impairment (the Sears smoke alarm). Intangibles add the sharpest asymmetry in GAAP: identical brands are a balance-sheet asset if bought and an expense if built. Every asset now reaches the statement through an estimate — which is precisely what Midterm II tests, and what quality-of-earnings analysis interrogates.

Liabilities & the time value of money · Ch. 9 · Classes 8–9 (Jul 27–29)

The course crosses to the right side of the balance sheet. Capital structure is the mix of debt (funds from creditors) and equity (funds from owners) financing the business — and debt is the riskier of the two for the firm, for exactly two reasons: interest is a legal obligation, and creditors can force bankruptcy. The fundamental questions for the chapter: whether, when, and how much to recognize as a liability. The definition is the Midterm I one — a probable future sacrifice of economic benefits arising from present obligations as a result of past events — and liabilities are recorded at their current cash equivalent: what a creditor would accept to settle today.

The split that organizes everything is maturity, and it carries a measurement rule with it:

The current-liability cast is familiar from Ch. 2–4: accounts payable (goods/services for operations), accrued liabilities (expenses incurred, not yet paid), notes payable (a formal written contract — and on a note, Interest = Principal × Rate × Time; the lender's revenue is the borrower's expense), and deferred/unearned revenues (cash received before earning it).

Warranties: the recorded contingency

A standard product warranty is the record-it cell of the grid: claims are probable and (from history) estimable. The mechanics are the allowance method's sibling — the professor's own note: "very similar in spirit to the returns allowance from L6." Warranty expense is accrued in the period of the related sale (matching, usually inside COGS), and actual claims later drain the liability without touching income:

Warranty payable roll-forward: Beg + Warranty expense (accrual) − Actual claims paid = End At sale: Dr Warranty Expense / Cr Warranty Payable (estimate) At claim: Dr Warranty Payable / Cr Cash / Inventory (actual)

The in-class problem (Perez Appliance): sales $800,000, warranty cost estimated at 3% of sales → expense $24,000 accrued at sale. Claims paid during the year $20,400 (the "$4,500 was for this year's products" detail is a distractor — the claim entry is the same regardless of which year's sale it services). Ending liability: 11,400 + 24,000 − 20,400 = $15,000. Apple's actual 10-K runs the same T-account in billions: beginning $3.69B + accruals $3.74B − claims $3.86B = ending $3.57B.

Why the chapter lingers here: an estimated liability is an earnings-management lever. The WSJ's distribution of S&P 500 earnings surprises shows almost nobody just misses and a huge spike just beats — and warranty accruals are one of the dials. Honda's warranty expense spiked to ~¥600B in 2016 (Takata airbags — a $22B industry recall bill) then fell back; Dell went further: it filed nothing with the SEC for 17 months, then restated four years of results (−$92M of net income), having used warranty accruals to overstate EPS by 2–8 cents in 5 of 12 quarters.

Time value of money: pricing the long-term side

How do you value a liability due years from now? A dollar today is worth more than a dollar later, for three reasons: people prefer present consumption, inflation erodes the dollar, and future cash is uncertain. The discount rate is the exchange rate between present and future money that prices all three in (aliases: interest rate, cost of capital, opportunity cost, required return). Converting forward is compounding; converting back is discounting.

Future value and present value

\[ FV_t = PV \times (1+r)^t \qquad\qquad PV = \frac{FV_t}{(1+r)^t} \]

One equation, four variables (PV, FV, r, t) — every problem gives three and asks for the fourth. Growth compounds: $1,000 at 5% is $1,050 after one year, but $1,102.50 after two — the extra $2.50 is interest on interest. Class examples: $25,000 in a retirement account at 6% for 30 years → 25,000 × 1.06³⁰ = $143,587; needing $100,000 for an MBA in 5 years at 6% → 100,000 ÷ 1.06⁵ = $74,726 today. The PV-of-$1 table is just 1/(1+r)ᵗ precomputed — e.g., 5 years at 10% → factor 0.621, so a $100,000 inheritance due in 5 years is worth ~$62,100 now.

The rate assumption dominates over long horizons. The class showpiece: Manhattan was "bought" in 1626 for 60 guilders (~$24). Compound $24 for 400 years — at 4% it's ~$156 million; at 5%, ~$7.2 billion; at 8%, ~$562 trillion. Same fact, three rates, twelve orders of magnitude. The Ibbotson data makes it practical: $100 for 40 years at stock-market returns (12.4%) → $10,731; at T-bill returns (3.6%) → $412. Higher rate → lower PV factor, because the money you set aside works harder.

Present value of an annuity (equal payments, equal periods)

\[ PV_{\text{annuity}} = \frac{1 - \dfrac{1}{(1+r)^t}}{r} \times \text{Payment} \]

An annuity is just a stack of single cash flows, and its factor is literally the sum of the individual PV factors: $100/year for 3 years at 10% = 100×0.909 + 100×0.826 + 100×0.751 = $248.60, or in one move 100 × 2.487 (the annuity factor = .909 + .826 + .751, off only by rounding). Scale it up: a trust paying $100,000/year for 10 years at 9% costs 100,000 × 6.418 = $641,800 today.

PAY ATTENTION TO THE NUMBER OF PERIODS — the professor's all-caps warning. Bond interest is generally semi-annual: divide the annual rate by 2 and double the periods. Getting $1,000 every 6 months for 3 years at a 10% annual rate = 1,000 × factor(t=6, r=5%) = $5,076 — worth more than $2,000 annually for 3 years ($4,974), because cash arrives sooner. This conversion is the setup skill for Ch. 10 bonds.

Bonds & long-term debt · Ch. 10 · Classes 9–10 (Jul 29–Aug 3)

Why debt at all? The pecking order (Myers & Majluf): firms prefer internal cash, then debt, then — last — issuing equity, because the cost of capital rises with information asymmetry. Even cash-rich Apple carries ~$100B of term debt. A bond is a loan in security form: the issuer borrows from many bondholders at once, usually via underwriters who buy the whole issue and resell it. The vocabulary splits into what's printed on the bond — face value (principal), coupon rate (r), maturity — and what the market decides: price and market rate / yield (m). Quote convention: a $1,000 bond "at 102" sells for $1,020.

The professor's biggest emphasis: the coupon rate is not an interest rate. It only sizes the periodic cash payment — use it once to compute the coupon, then "throw it away." The market rate at issuance does everything else: prices the bond and drives interest expense every period. Rate changes after issuance never touch interest expense. This is accrual accounting again: expense accrues independent of the cash outflow.

Bond price = two present values (always at the market rate)

\[ \text{Price} = \underbrace{\frac{\text{Face}}{(1+m)^n}}_{\text{principal at maturity}} + \underbrace{\text{Coupon} \times \text{PVA}(n, m)}_{\text{annuity of coupons}} \]

A bond is just the Ch. 9 toolkit applied twice: one lump sum plus one annuity, both discounted at what the market demands (m), never at what the paper promises (r). Semiannual coupons — the norm — mean n = years × 2 and rate = m ÷ 2. The class example: 10-year, $1,000 face, 12% coupon paid semiannually, market at 10% → PV of face = 1,000 × 0.3769 = $376.90, plus coupons of $60 × 12.4622 = $747.73 → price $1,124.63. It sells above face precisely because the coupon pays more than the market requires.

That's the whole premium/discount story in one table: same bond, market at 10% → $1,125 (premium); at 12% → $1,000 (par); at 14% → $894 (discount). Price > face when r > m, < face when r < m. And the key takeaway slide: discounts and premiums are an accounting response to market rates, not a verdict on the issuer's creditworthiness — they're just different contractual timings of when the interest gets paid in cash.

Issuance entries: the plug is a (contra-)account

Bonds Payable is always credited at face; the gap between face and proceeds lands in its own account. The GM class pair ($100,000, 5-year, 9% coupon semiannual):

Market 12% → price $88,960 (discount): Market 7% → price $108,317 (premium): Dr Cash 88,960 Dr Cash 108,317 Dr Discount on Bonds Payable 11,040 Cr Premium on Bonds Payable 8,317 Cr Bonds Payable 100,000 Cr Bonds Payable 100,000

Each period after: interest expense = beginning book value × market rate per period (the effective-interest method), cash coupon = face × r per period, and the difference amortizes the discount/premium so book value walks toward face by maturity. On the balance sheet the bond sits at face − discount (or + premium) — the same "net of a valuation account" pattern as A/R and PP&E.

Zero-coupon bonds

No coupons at all → the price is just the PV of the principal, so zeros always issue at a deep discount. Class example: GM's 10-year, $20M face zero at 10% annual → proceeds 20,000,000 × 0.3855 = $7,710,000 (Dr Cash 7,710,000, Dr Discount 12,290,000 / Cr Bonds Payable 20,000,000). Interest expense each year = 10% × book value ($771,000 in year 1, $848,100 in year 2 as the balance grows) — no cash until the $20M at maturity. Watch the periods: annual compounding here, not semiannual, because there's no semiannual coupon forcing the split. (The straight-line-vs-effective tax comparison from the 1985 Treasury rule is FYI.)

Credit ratings & default risk

Default = missing a promised payment, and size doesn't protect you: Neiman Marcus, with $6B+ of assets, defaulted on a $5.7M coupon in 2020 and went through bankruptcy. Rating agencies (S&P/Moody's/Fitch) grade default risk: investment grade runs AAA → BBB (Moody's: Aaa → Baa), speculative runs BB → C, and D means default. Lower rating → higher yield demanded, with the BBB–AAA spread blowing out in recessions. Subtlety worth knowing: agencies rewrite the GAAP statements before rating — adding back leases, netting cash against debt — and the Kraft (2015) study shows they adjust nearly every firm's debt up and income down (3M: reported metrics said Aa1, adjusted said Aa2).

Covenants — and the games around them

Debt covenants are the contract's teeth: they can require actions (audited statements, insurance), restrict actions (M&A, dividends, new debt), or mandate financial conditions (max leverage, min current ratio). "EBITDA" in a credit agreement is a heavily negotiated defined term with pages of add-backs. The live example: Campbell Soup's debt-to-EBITDA covenant stepped down to 5.75x by April 2020 — and in June 2019 they sat at 5.95x, above the line. Managers near thresholds manage the numbers: reclassify debt, keep it off balance sheet, or — the professor's own research (Aleszczyk 2019) — repay short-term bonds just before quarter-end and reborrow after; the data shows bond counts dipping to a minimum exactly at the reporting date.

The credit-analysis ratio kit

\[ \frac{\text{Total Liabilities}}{\text{Equity}} \quad \frac{\text{Total Liabilities}}{\text{Assets}} \quad \underbrace{\frac{\text{EBIT or EBITDA}}{\text{Interest Expense}}}_{\text{interest coverage}} \quad \frac{\text{Total Debt}}{\text{EBITDA}} \]

Two policy ratios (how much of the firm creditors fund — "total liabilities" can be narrowed to just ST + LT debt) and two strength ratios (can the cash flows service it). EBITDA = EBIT + D&A, with D&A pulled from the cash flow statement. The in-class Campbell's workout (FY2025): debt/equity 2.82x, debt/assets 73.8% — creditors fund three-quarters of the company; coverage = 1,558/345 = 4.52x, debt/EBITDA = 6,857/1,558 = 4.40x. The FY24→FY25 move is the exam-worthy subtlety: debt-to-EBITDA improved (debt fell, EBITDA rose) while coverage worsened — interest expense jumped 39% on acquisition debt. Two strength ratios, opposite verdicts, same year.

Early retirement (FYI): when a company calls its bonds, gain/loss = book value − cash paid — the disposal logic from Ch. 8 transplanted to the liability side. The Amazon example: bonds carried at $101,886 bought back at their market value of $98,167 (rates had risen to 12%, sinking the price) → Dr Bonds Payable 100,000, Dr Premium 1,886 / Cr Cash 98,167, Cr Gain 3,719. Rising rates hand issuers repurchase gains.

state of the equation · after ch. 10 \[ \text{Bond}_{\text{B/S}} = \text{Face} \pm \text{Premium/Discount} \qquad \text{Int. Exp.} = BV_{beg} \times m \neq \text{Coupon} = \text{Face} \times r \]

New: long-term debt gets the full valuation treatment — a bond enters the books at its market-priced PV, sits at face net of a premium/discount account, and unwinds via the effective-interest method, which finally splits "interest" into two independent numbers: cash paid (coupon, fixed by contract) and expense (market rate × book value, fixed at issuance forever). The right side now mirrors the left's big lesson: recorded values are frozen at issuance-date market conditions, which is why rate moves create retirement gains/losses and why rating agencies re-derive the numbers before trusting them. Income statement so far: interest expense is now a schedule that can exceed or trail the cash coupon — the last wedge between income and cash flow this course adds.

Stockholders' equity · Ch. 11 · Class 11 (Aug 5)

Final-exam scope, straight from the slides' red ink: Basic EPS is "in the final"; diluted EPS is "ignore for final." Stock splits are "relevant"; small stock dividends "meh", large ones "okay"; stock options "ignore" (FYI), OCI FYI. The last stop: the residual claim itself. E = A − L, and the section's anatomy is contributed capital (common stock at par + APIC, plus preferred stock) + retained earnings − treasury stock (contra) ± accumulated OCI. Versus debt: stockholders vote, dividends are optional and never an expense (no tax deduction — interest gets one), and there's no fixed claim. Preferred stock & the dividend waterfall

Preferred = debt costume, equity law: a stated dividend (rate × par), priority over common, usually no vote — but nothing is owed until declared. Cumulative preferred accrues skipped dividends as arrears that must be cleared before common sees a cent (arrears are a footnote, not a liability). The class allocation table (7% × $10 par × 10,000 = $7,000/yr preference): a $10,000 dividend after a $2,000 arrears year splits 9,000 preferred / 1,000 common; a skipped year rolls forward in full. Non-cumulative skips vanish forever.

Share counts & treasury stock

Authorized ≥ issued ≥ outstanding; outstanding = issued − treasury. Treasury stock is a contra-equity debit at cost — a company cannot own itself and call it an asset. Amazon's balance sheet made it concrete: 23M treasury shares carried at $1.837B = $79.87/share bought back years ago, frozen at historical cost while the stock traded at thousands. The three commandments from the slides: no journal entry when your stock trades in the market; a company never records gains or losses on its own stock — reissue differences hit APIC (Dr APIC if reissued below cost; RE only if APIC runs dry); and buybacks never touch Common Stock, APIC, or RE. The Strait Corp drill: buy 1,000 at $16, resell 500 at $30 → Cr APIC 7,000; resell 500 at $15 → Dr APIC 500.

Why buy back? Return capital, support the price, fund comp plans, block takeovers — and reason #6 from the slide, "artificially inflate EPS." The IBM breakout proved it: FY2017 EPS was $6.16 with the year's 28M-share repurchase, $6.07 without. Same income, better ratio. (Buybacks have eaten about half of S&P 500 free cash flow since 2010.)

Basic EPS (in the final — diluted is not)

\[ \text{Basic EPS} = \frac{\text{Net Income} - \text{Preferred Dividends}}{\text{Weighted-avg common shares outstanding}} \]

Income earned for common per common share — so preferred dividends come out of the numerator, and the denominator is time-weighted: shares count for the fraction of the year they were outstanding. The Hanson example: NI 443 − 12 preferred = 431; shares 213M for half the year, 196M for a quarter (after a 17M buyback), 199M for the last quarter (3M reissued) → 0.5×213 + 0.25×196 + 0.25×199 = 205.25M → EPS $2.10. Buybacks mid-year shrink the average immediately — that's the EPS-inflation lever in the formula.

The payout ratio kit

\[ \text{Payout} = \frac{\text{Div} + \text{Repurchases}}{\text{NI}} \qquad \text{Div. Yield} = \frac{\text{DPS}}{\text{Price}_{beg}} \qquad \text{ROE} = \frac{\text{NI}}{\text{Avg Equity}} \]

Year-end, managers face one fork: return net income to shareholders or retain it (retention ratio = 1 − payout). Dividend yield is the cash half of a stock's return (price change is the other) — and the red-ink detail: divide by the beginning-of-period price (Apple 2019: $3 ÷ ~$150 ≈ 2%; by 2026, $1.08 ÷ $300 ≈ 0.3% — growth repriced the yield away). ROE is the accounting bottom line of the whole course: how efficiently the residual claim turns equity into earnings.
